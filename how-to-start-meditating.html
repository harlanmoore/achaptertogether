A beginner's guide

How to start meditating

You don't need to empty your mind, sit on the floor, or commit to 30 minutes a day. Here's what meditation actually involves — and a gentle way to begin this week.

Most people have a slightly wrong picture of what meditation is. They imagine it involves sitting very still, clearing the mind of all thought, and achieving some kind of inner blank. None of that is quite right — and the misunderstanding is probably why so many attempts at meditation quietly fizzle after a few days.

What meditation actually is

Meditation is simply the practice of paying attention — usually to the breath, the body, or the sounds around you — and noticing when your mind has wandered, then gently bringing it back. That returning is the practice. The wandering is not a failure; it's what minds do. The moment you notice you've drifted and come back is the equivalent of lifting a weight at the gym. It's the exercise itself.

There's no special state to achieve. No emptiness to reach. A session in which your mind wandered forty times and returned forty times is a good session. You practised forty times.

What the research shows

A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis in PMC, examining 30 randomised controlled trials across more than 24,000 participants, found that mindfulness-based interventions produced moderate to strong effects on reducing anxiety, depression, and stress. A 2025 study from the University of Southern California found that even short daily practice improved attention control in adults of all ages. The Surgeon General's 2023 advisory on loneliness cited meditation as one of the approaches with evidence for reducing social isolation.

None of this requires years of practice. Several studies show meaningful effects from programmes as short as four to eight weeks — sometimes less.

What you actually need

  • ✓
    A comfortable chair.Not a cushion on the floor. Not a special posture. Just somewhere you can sit upright without slumping, with your feet flat on the ground and your hands resting in your lap.
  • ✓
    Five minutes.Five minutes done consistently is worth more than an hour done once and abandoned. Start small enough that it feels easy, then extend if you want to.
  • ✓
    Nothing else.No app, no class, no special equipment. A timer on a phone (faced down so you can't watch it) is the only tool you need.

A simple first practice

Your first five-minute meditation

  1. Sit comfortably in your chair. Close your eyes, or let your gaze rest softly on the floor a few feet ahead of you.
  2. Take one slow breath in through the nose, and out through the mouth. Then let your breathing return to its natural pace — don't control it.
  3. Bring your attention to the feeling of breathing. The cool air coming in at the nostrils. The gentle rise and fall of the chest. Just notice it.
  4. When you notice your mind has wandered — to a thought, a worry, a memory, a plan — that's fine. Just notice that it wandered, and gently bring your attention back to the breath. No commentary, no frustration. Just back.
  5. Continue for five minutes. When the timer sounds, open your eyes slowly and sit for a moment before getting up.

Common questions beginners ask

"My mind won't stop — does that mean I'm doing it wrong?"
No. A busy mind is a normal mind. The instruction is not to have no thoughts, but to notice when you're lost in thought and return. Noticing is the skill.

"Should I follow a guided recording?"
Many beginners find a gentle voice helpful — it gives the mind something to follow rather than generating its own commentary. Our guide for seniors specifically covers some free options. But the unguided breath practice above is complete in itself.

"How do I know if it's working?"
You may not notice anything during the session. The effects — a slightly calmer mood, a little more patience, a bit more space between an impulse and a reaction — tend to show up in daily life rather than on the cushion. Give it two weeks before you judge.

"What time of day is best?"
The one you'll actually do. Morning before the day gets busy is a common choice because it's less likely to get displaced. But shortly before bed is also useful, especially if sleep is the goal.

Keeping it going

The biggest obstacle to meditation is not difficulty — it's forgetting. The practice feels subtle enough that it's easy to skip, and the effects are gradual enough that you don't immediately notice when you stop.

A few things help: tying your practice to something you already do reliably (morning coffee, brushing teeth), keeping it short enough that there's no excuse to skip, and not trying to increase the time too quickly. Five minutes every day for a month will transform more than thirty minutes done once a week.
