Managing anxiety

Meditation for anxiety

Anxiety often lives in the future — the what-ifs, the worries, the running commentary. Meditation practises returning to the present. Here's what the evidence shows and how to begin.

Anxiety has a particular quality: it almost always lives in the future, or in the past. The mind rehearses things that might go wrong, or replays things that did. Meditation offers something simple but powerful in response — it practises returning to right now, where most of the feared things are not actually happening.

What the research shows

The evidence base for mindfulness and anxiety is now substantial. A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis in PMC, covering 30 randomised controlled trials and more than 24,000 participants, found moderate to strong effects from mindfulness-based interventions on anxiety reduction. A separate review in PMC from 2025 with comprehensive coverage of the literature confirmed that mindfulness programmes consistently outperformed control conditions on anxiety measures across diverse populations.

Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR), developed by Jon Kabat-Zinn at the University of Massachusetts, is the most studied programme — a structured eight-week course that has been tested in hundreds of clinical trials. But shorter, informal practices have also shown meaningful effects, particularly for people who are new to meditation or prefer a less structured approach.

An honest note: Meditation is a complement to professional care, not a substitute. If anxiety is significantly affecting your daily life, please also speak with your doctor or a mental health professional. Many people find meditation most effective when used alongside other support, not instead of it.

Why anxiety specifically responds to meditation

Anxiety is partly maintained by two mental habits: rumination (going over the same worrying thoughts repeatedly) and avoidance (trying not to feel anxious, which paradoxically amplifies anxiety). Meditation addresses both.

By practising the observation of thoughts without engaging with them — noticing that a worried thought has arisen, without following it into a spiral — meditators gradually develop a different relationship with their anxiety. The thought is still there, but the pull to engage with it weakens. There's a small but real space between the anxious thought and the anxious reaction.

This is sometimes called "decentering" or "cognitive defusion" in psychological research — the ability to see thoughts as mental events rather than as facts about reality.

Techniques that work particularly well for anxiety

1. Breath focus

The most basic and most studied approach. Bring attention to the breath — the sensation at the nostrils, the movement of the chest or belly. When anxiety-related thoughts arise (as they will), notice them and return to the breath. The breath is always happening in the present moment, which is why it's such a reliable anchor.

2. Body scan

Slowly moving attention through different parts of the body — feet, legs, torso, arms, face — and simply noticing physical sensations without trying to change them. Anxiety often manifests physically (tightness in the chest, tension in the shoulders) and the body scan helps develop a non-reactive awareness of these sensations, which tends to reduce their intensity.

3. Noting

A slightly more active technique: as thoughts, feelings, or sensations arise, gently name them — "thinking," "worrying," "planning," "remembering" — before returning to the breath. The naming creates a gentle separation between you and the thought. Useful for particularly active or anxious minds.

A five-minute practice for an anxious moment

  1. Sit or lie comfortably. If it helps, place one hand on your chest and one on your belly.
  2. Take three slow, deliberate breaths — in for four counts, hold for two, out for six. This activates the parasympathetic nervous system and begins to slow the anxiety response.
  3. Let your breathing return to its natural rhythm. Bring attention to whichever sensation is most noticeable — the breath at the nostrils, the rise and fall of the belly, the warmth of your hands.
  4. When a worried thought arrives (it will), mentally note "worrying" and gently return to the breath. No struggle, no argument with the thought.
  5. Continue for five minutes. Notice how you feel afterwards — not expecting drama, just noticing.

A note on consistency

The research shows that regular short practice tends to be more effective than occasional long sessions. Even five minutes daily for four weeks produces measurable changes in how the mind responds to stressful situations. The goal is not to be unmoved by anxiety, but to have a slightly different relationship with it — more choice, less automatic reaction.
