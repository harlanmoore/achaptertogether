Meditation for seniors: a gentle starting point

Later life is, in many ways, an excellent time to begin meditating. Here's a gentle, practical introduction written specifically for older adults — no experience, no special equipment needed.

There is a particular irony in the way meditation is often marketed. It tends to feature young, athletic people in expensive studios — which sends entirely the wrong message. The practice is, if anything, better suited to later life than to early adulthood. Here's why, and how to begin.

Why later life is actually a good time to start

Meditation is fundamentally about attention — the capacity to be present with what's actually here, rather than lost in thought about what was or might be. And the qualities that support this practice tend to mature with age: patience, perspective, a greater capacity to let things be as they are, a clearer sense of what actually matters.

Several studies have confirmed that older adults benefit from mindfulness practice just as much as younger ones. A 2025 study from the University of Southern California found that short daily practice improved attention control across all age groups. Research from the University of Michigan's MBSR loneliness study — summarised in our broader guide to meditation benefits — found that an eight-week programme reduced loneliness in older adults. You are not too old to start, and you are not starting too late.

Common concerns — answered honestly

  • ?
    "My mind is too busy to meditate."This is the most common concern — and it's based on a misunderstanding of what meditation involves. The practice isn't about having a quiet mind. It's about noticing when the mind is busy, and gently returning. A busy mind gives you more opportunities to practise returning. That's all.
  • ?
    "I can't sit on the floor."You don't need to. A straight-backed chair is ideal. You can also practise lying down, though the risk of falling asleep increases. There is nothing sacred about the floor.
  • ?
    "Is this religious?"The meditation taught in clinical and scientific settings — and offered through A Chapter Together — is entirely secular. The practices are drawn from mindfulness research, not from any specific religious tradition. People of all faiths, and no faith, practise them.
  • ?
    "I tried it once and fell asleep."That's fine — it means you were relaxed, which has its own value. If you want to stay awake, sit slightly more upright, keep your eyes open with a soft downward gaze, or practise earlier in the day rather than when you're tired.
  • ?
    "I'm dealing with real problems — will this actually help?"Meditation is not a cure for difficult circumstances. But a substantial body of research shows it helps with the mental and physical effects of those circumstances: anxiety, low mood, disrupted sleep, loneliness, and the physical consequences of chronic stress. It works alongside real problems, not instead of addressing them.

Adapting the practice to physical limitations

If you have chronic pain, limited mobility, or conditions that make sitting uncomfortable, meditation can be adapted. The breath — or any sensation — can be the focus whether you're sitting, lying, or even walking slowly. Several studies have found that gentle movement-based practices like tai chi have many of the same effects as seated mindfulness, for people who find stillness difficult.

Hearing difficulties don't prevent meditation — the breath is always available, and internal awareness doesn't depend on sound. Vision difficulties are irrelevant — most practice is done with eyes closed or cast downward.

A first practice you can try today

Five minutes in your favourite chair

  1. Sit comfortably in a chair you like. Feet flat on the floor, hands resting in your lap. No need to sit particularly upright — just comfortably alert.
  2. Close your eyes, or look down at the floor a few feet ahead. Take one deliberate, slow breath in through the nose, out through the mouth.
  3. Let your breathing return to normal, and bring your attention to wherever you feel the breath most clearly — the nostrils, the chest, or the belly. Stay there.
  4. Each time you notice your mind has wandered (which will happen many times — that's completely normal), gently return to the breath. No commentary, no frustration. Just back.
  5. Do this for five minutes. When the time is up, open your eyes gently and take a moment before standing.

Free resources to continue

If you'd like to continue on your own, Senior Planet from AARP (seniorplanet.org, 1-888-713-3495) offers free virtual classes including meditation and mindfulness sessions, designed specifically for older adults. Many local libraries also run gentle mindfulness classes — worth asking at your nearest branch.
