A practical guide

Activities for seniors who live alone

Not all activities are equal. These ones are chosen because they actually reduce loneliness, maintain health, or provide lasting purpose — not just fill time.

Living alone doesn't have to mean an empty day. But filling time and genuinely enriching it are different things — and most lists of "activities for seniors" fail to make that distinction. This one tries to.

The activities below are chosen for a specific reason: they either reduce loneliness, maintain cognitive or physical health, or provide the kind of purpose and engagement that makes a day feel well-spent rather than just passed. Some you can do alone; most work better with at least one other person.

Activities built around connection

Research consistently shows that the quality of social connection matters more than the quantity. A rushed daily phone call does less than one meaningful weekly conversation. Activities that build genuine connection — not just contact — are the most valuable.

  • 1
    A regular reading partnership.Reading the same book as someone else — whether in person, on video, or even just exchanging chapters by phone — gives every call a ready-made subject. The conversation about the book often becomes the most engaged, natural talking you do all week.
  • 2
    A standing weekly call with a specific person.Not a general "call more often" — a specific person, the same time each week. Predictability is part of what makes it valuable. Both people know it's coming, which means both people think of things to share during the week.
  • 3
    A local or online interest group.Book clubs, film discussion groups, gardening clubs, history societies, craft circles. The shared activity takes the pressure off the socialising — you always have something to talk about.
  • 4
    Volunteering for something specific.Helping at a library, reading to children at a school, mentoring younger workers, serving at a food bank. Volunteering restores a sense of purpose and places you alongside people of all ages who are doing something they care about. National Institute on Aging research has found that activity programs involving purpose and social engagement measurably improve mood and quality of life in older adults.
  • 5
    Teaching something you know.Do you cook, garden, sew, play an instrument, speak another language, or know a trade? Teaching — formally or informally, to grandchildren or local young people — is one of the most meaningful forms of connection. You're not just spending time; you're passing something on.

Activities that keep the mind sharp

A study of 294 older adults published in Neurology found that frequent engagement in cognitively stimulating activities reduced the rate of memory decline by 32%. The activities that worked best were those that engaged active thinking — not passive consumption.

  • 6
    Reading — especially challenging or unfamiliar material.Fiction, biography, history, science writing — the point is active engagement with ideas, not comfort reading (though comfort reading has its place too). Challenging your understanding of something new is better for the brain than retreading familiar ground.
  • 7
    Learning something genuinely new.A language, an instrument, a craft you've never tried, a subject you always meant to explore. Online learning platforms — many free or low-cost — put an extraordinary range of courses within reach. The combination of novelty and sustained effort is particularly good for cognitive health.
  • 8
    Writing — letters, memoir, or a journal.Handwriting letters is a meaningful act of connection (and people love receiving them). Keeping a journal is one of the most evidence-backed interventions for emotional wellbeing and self-understanding. Writing a memoir — even informally, just for family — combines reflection, narrative, and legacy.
  • 9
    Games with a mental challenge.Chess, bridge, crosswords, Scrabble, jigsaw puzzles, strategy games. The social version is better than the solo version — even if it's just a crossword you compare answers on by phone — because the social dimension adds an extra layer of engagement.
  • 10
    Current affairs and discussion.Following the news, listening to podcasts, reading long-form journalism — and having someone to discuss it with. Opinion and debate exercise the mind differently from absorbing information. A regular "what did you think of that?" conversation is more cognitively demanding than just watching the news.

Physical activities that also connect

Movement matters for its own sake — falls are the leading cause of injury in adults over 65, and staying physically active is the single most consistent predictor of healthy ageing. But the social version of physical activity consistently produces better outcomes than the solo version.

  • 11
    A regular walking companion or group.Walking has a remarkable range of benefits — cardiovascular, mood, balance, bone density — but walking with another person or a group also means conversation, accountability, and something to look forward to. Local walking groups for seniors are more common than people realise; your Area Agency on Aging can help find one.
  • 12
    Tai chi or gentle yoga — ideally in a group.Both are particularly recommended for older adults because they improve balance and reduce fall risk, while being low-impact and manageable at most fitness levels. A class, rather than a solo DVD, also provides a weekly social anchor.
  • 13
    Gardening.Physical, purposeful, structured by seasons, and naturally connective — neighbours talk over fences, community gardens put you alongside other people, and the results are shareable. One of the most consistently cited activities among older adults as meaningful and enjoyable.
  • 14
    Swimming or water aerobics.Particularly good for older adults with arthritis or joint pain, because the water removes impact. Many community pools run senior-specific sessions, which again combine the physical benefit with a regular social appointment.

Creative and expressive activities

  • 15
    Art — drawing, painting, ceramics, textile work.Creative activities improve fine motor skills, boost confidence, and provide a sense of accomplishment. Group art classes (many senior centres and libraries run them) add a social dimension. The output is also shareable — grandchildren, family, and friends respond well to something made.
  • 16
    Music — listening deeply, or playing.Music and memory are deeply intertwined. An older adult who can no longer easily recall recent events will often remember lyrics and melodies from 50 years ago. Playing an instrument — even one picked up later in life — provides a sustained challenge that is excellent for the brain.
  • 17
    Photographing and organising family history.Going through old photographs, labelling them, sharing them with younger family members, or creating a physical or digital album is meaningful to the whole family and gives the older adult a purposeful, absorbing project that connects to people they love.

A note on what makes activities actually stick

The activities most likely to become regular habits share a few features: they're built around something the person genuinely enjoys (not something they think they should enjoy), they involve at least some social element, and they create a reason to show up again next week. An activity that checks all the right boxes on paper but that you dread is worth less than one that's modest in scope but that you genuinely look forward to.

Start with one. The goal isn't a full calendar. It's one good thing each week that feels worth doing, with someone who's glad to be there too.
