Activities for seniors who live alone — the ones that actually help.

Most lists of activities for older adults are lists of ways to pass an afternoon. This one is sorted by a harder question: does it make the week feel less empty?

By Harlan Moore · 7 min read · Updated July 2026

If you've searched for activities for a parent who lives alone, you've already found the lists. Puzzles. Bird feeders. Adult colouring books. They're not wrong, exactly. They're just answering a different question than the one you're actually asking.

Filling a day and enriching one are not the same thing. A person can be busy from breakfast to bedtime and still be lonely at the end of it. So the activities below are sorted by what they actually do: ease loneliness, protect the mind or body, or give a week its shape. Some can be done alone. Most work better with at least one other person in them — and that's the whole point.

Activities built around connection

The research here is consistent and slightly counterintuitive: the quality of contact matters more than the amount. A rushed daily phone call does less good than one unhurried conversation a week. If you only change one thing on this list, change something here.

  • A reading partnership. Two people reading the same book — in the same room, over video, or just trading chapters by phone — gives every conversation somewhere to go. You stop asking "how are you feeling?" and start arguing about whether the ending worked. For a lot of families, that's the most natural talking they've done in years.
  • A standing call with one specific person. Not "call more often." A person, a day, a time. The predictability is doing most of the work: both people know it's coming, so both people save things up during the week to tell each other.
  • An interest group, local or online. Book clubs, film discussions, gardening clubs, history societies, craft circles. The shared subject takes the pressure off the socialising — nobody has to make conversation, because the conversation is already there.
  • Volunteering for something specific. Shelving at the library, reading to children at a school, a shift at the food bank. Volunteering does two things at once: it restores a sense of being needed, and it puts your parent alongside people of every age who care about the same thing they do.
  • Teaching what they already know. Cooking, gardening, sewing, an instrument, a language, a trade. Teaching — even informally, even just to a grandchild on a Sunday — is one of the few activities that makes a person feel like they're passing something on rather than passing the time.

Activities that keep the mind sharp

A study of 294 older adults published in Neurology found that frequent engagement in cognitively stimulating activity was associated with a 32% slower rate of memory decline. The activities that did the work were the ones that required active thinking, not passive watching.

  • Reading, especially the unfamiliar. Fiction, biography, history, science writing. Comfort reading has its place — but a book that makes you work is doing something a re-read of an old favourite isn't.
  • Learning something genuinely new. A language, an instrument, a craft they never got round to. Novelty plus sustained effort is the combination that matters, and a great deal of it is free online now.
  • Writing letters, a journal, or a memoir. Handwritten letters are a small act of connection, and people love receiving them. Memoir — even scrappy, even just for the family — combines reflection, narrative and legacy in one project. Most parents will say nobody would want to read it. Most families do.
  • Games with a real challenge in them. Chess, bridge, crosswords, Scrabble, jigsaws. The two-player version beats the solo one every time — even if it's just comparing crossword answers by phone.
  • Following something, and having someone to argue with about it. News, podcasts, long-form journalism. Forming an opinion is harder mental work than absorbing information, and "what did you make of that?" is a better weekly ritual than the news itself.

Movement — ideally with someone else

Falls are the leading cause of injury in adults over 65, and staying active is the most reliable predictor of ageing well. But the group version of exercise consistently outperforms the solo version, and not only because people actually show up.

  • A walking companion or walking group. Walking does an unreasonable amount of good — heart, mood, balance, bone density — and walking with someone adds conversation and a reason to be dressed by ten. Senior walking groups are more common than most people realise; an Area Agency on Aging can usually point to one.
  • Tai chi or gentle yoga, in a class. Both improve balance and lower fall risk at almost any fitness level. A class rather than a video also puts a fixed social appointment in the week.
  • Gardening. Physical, purposeful, structured by seasons, and quietly sociable — neighbours talk over fences, community plots put people side by side, and the results are worth giving away.
  • Swimming or water aerobics. The water takes the impact out, which makes it one of the few options that stays open to people with arthritis or joint pain. Most community pools run senior sessions, which again bundles the exercise with the company.

Making something

  • Art — drawing, painting, ceramics, textiles. Fine motor skills, confidence, and something to show for the afternoon. Libraries and senior centres run classes, which adds the social half.
  • Music, listened to closely or played. Music and memory are wound tightly together — someone who can't recall Tuesday will still have every word of a song from 1961. Picking up an instrument late is a genuine cognitive challenge, and nobody is too old to be a beginner.
  • Sorting the family photographs. Labelling them, dating them, scanning them, telling the grandchildren who everyone is. It's absorbing, it's finite, it matters to the whole family, and it's one of the rare projects where the older adult is the only person who can do it.

What makes an activity actually stick

The ones that last share three things. They're built on something your parent genuinely likes, not something they think they ought to like. They have at least one other person in them. And they create a reason to show up again next week.

An activity that ticks every box on paper but that your mother quietly dreads is worth less than a modest one she looks forward to. Enjoyment isn't a bonus feature here. It's the mechanism.

The goal isn't a full calendar. It's one good thing a week, with someone who's glad to be there too.

How to suggest one without it landing as homework

This is where most of us go wrong. We research well, then present the finding — and it arrives as a management decision. A few things that help:

  • Offer two, not one. A choice is participation. A single suggestion is an assignment.
  • Let them pick the day. Small, but it's the difference between their activity and yours.
  • Make no easy. "Try it once, and if you hate it we drop it" removes the thing they're actually worried about, which is being stuck with it to spare your feelings.
  • Say why you want it. "I'd feel better knowing there's something in your week" is honest, and it lets them do you a favour rather than accept one. Parents are often far more willing to help you than to be helped.

Then start with one. Not a schedule — one thing, one day a week, and give it three tries before you judge it. First sessions of anything are awkward. By the third, you'll usually hear the difference in their voice before they say a word about it.

Common questions

Questions families ask.

What are good activities for seniors who live alone?

The ones with another person in them: a standing weekly call, a reading partnership, a walking group, a book club, teaching something they know. Solo activities — reading, writing, gardening, music — are genuinely good for the mind and body, but they don't touch loneliness on their own. Aim for one recurring thing a week that involves someone else.

How do I keep my parent busy without it feeling like I'm managing them?

Don't aim for busy. Aim for one good thing a week. Offer two options rather than one, let them pick the day, and make it easy to say no. An activity your parent chose will outlast one you booked for them, even when yours looked better on paper.

What helps with loneliness specifically, as opposed to boredom?

Loneliness responds to depth and predictability, not volume. One unhurried weekly conversation with the same person does more than daily check-in calls. Shared activities help because they take the pressure off the talking — a book, a walk or a garden gives the conversation somewhere to go.
