How to help a lonely parent — without making it awkward.

A short, honest guide for adult children. What actually helps, what usually backfires, and how to bring it up without making things worse.

By Harlan Moore · 8 min read · Updated July 2026

Nobody wants to be the child who calls to say, "Mom, I think you're lonely." So most of us don't. We call more, feel guilty, and hope she'll bring it up herself. She usually won't.

I wrote this because I've been on both sides — as a son who lived overseas from a parent I worried about, and as someone who now runs a companionship service for families in exactly this situation. Here's what I've learned about what actually helps.

First, what usually doesn't work

Before we get to the things that do help, a short list of the moves most of us try first — and why they tend to fall flat.

  • Telling them they should get out more. They know. They probably tried. Advice from a distance often lands as judgment, even when it isn't meant that way.
  • Giving them a tablet with 12 new apps. Technology alone rarely solves loneliness. If they weren't going to reach out before, an app won't change that — and now they feel bad about the tablet, too.
  • Booking them into a class they didn't ask for. Group activities can be wonderful, but they need to want them. Signing your parent up for pottery on Tuesday can feel less like a gift and more like a homework assignment.
  • Calling more often, but for less time each call. Frequent short calls can start to feel like check-ins — you're making sure they're alive, not spending time with them. Fewer, longer calls tend to feel more like company.

None of these are wrong exactly. They just tend to skip past the actual problem, which isn't a scheduling issue. It's the absence of someone who's genuinely glad to be there.

What actually helps

1. Name it gently, without diagnosing them

You don't have to sit down and say "Mom, are you lonely?" Try something softer: "The days must feel long sometimes." Or "I've been thinking about how quiet the house must be." You're not asking for an answer. You're making room for one.

What you're really doing is telling them: I see it. You don't have to pretend with me. That alone changes something.

2. Ask them what they miss, not what they need

"What do you need?" is a hard question at any age. Most people, especially proud ones, will say "nothing." But "What do you miss?" opens a door. They might say — a friend who moved, a conversation with someone new, an evening walk with your dad, a book club they were in for twenty years, the sound of the house when the kids were still home.

Those answers point straight at what actually helps. And they help you avoid the trap of solving the wrong problem.

3. Add one recurring thing they can look forward to

Loneliness is worst when the week has no shape. A single reliable thing — same day, same time — changes the feeling of the whole week. It doesn't have to be big. A Sunday call with a specific grandchild. A Wednesday lunch with a neighbor. A weekly video session with someone who reads with them.

The point is not the activity. The point is the shape it puts on the week: "Tuesday is my day." That sentence, said with real feeling, is what we're aiming for.

4. Bring in someone who isn't you

This is the honest part. You cannot be the only warm voice in your parent's life, and you shouldn't try to be. It's not fair to them — one person can only carry so much — and it's not fair to you. Your marriage, your work, and your own life need you too.

The parents who do best have several sources of warmth. Family. Friends. A neighbor. A regular helper. And, increasingly, a paid companion who's there specifically to spend time with them — not to run errands, not to clean, but to sit and talk. That's the gap our service was built to fill, and it's the gap most families feel most sharply.

5. Give them agency in the fix

Whatever you introduce, let your parent choose. Present two options, not one. Let them pick the day. Let them say no to the first companion and try another. The moment they feel like something is being done to them, they'll resist — and rightly so.

The goal isn't to fix them. It's to make room for them to have their own life again.

How to bring it up

The best moment isn't a phone call. It's an in-person visit, or the tail end of a long, unhurried conversation. Something like:

"I was thinking about how much of your week is quiet. I don't want to tell you what to do, but there's something I'd like to try — for me as much as for you. It's a weekly visit with a companion. I'll set it up, first one is free, and if you hate it we drop it. Would you be willing?"

Three things this line does well: it names your motivation honestly ("for me as much as for you"), it removes the risk ("first one is free"), and it gives them the exit ("if you hate it we drop it"). Very few parents refuse when it's framed this way.

What to expect once it's set up

The first session is usually tentative. Your parent may say afterwards that it was "fine." That's often what people say about things they enjoyed but aren't ready to admit are helping. Give it three sessions. By the third, you'll usually hear it in their voice — the day is a little different now.

If it isn't working, say so. A different companion, a different day, a different format. Real relationships take a couple of tries sometimes. That's not a failure of the idea; it's just how people work.

A last thought

Loneliness in a parent isn't a problem you solve once and move on from. It's a slow-moving thing you tend to. But tending it makes a real difference — to their health, their mood, their years — and it also changes your relationship with them, because you're no longer the one voice trying to hold everything up.

The best gift you can give a lonely parent isn't more of your time. It's a life around them that has more warm voices in it than just yours.

Harlan Moore is the founder of A Chapter Together, a companionship service for older adults. He has fifteen years of meditation practice and a family background in clinical psychology that shapes how our companions are trained.
