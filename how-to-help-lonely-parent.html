How to help a lonely parent who lives alone

Practical, honest advice — including what the research says actually works, and what tends to fall short despite good intentions.

Realising your parent is lonely is one of those quiet, creeping discoveries — not a single moment but a growing pattern. The calls that mention no one they've seen. The enthusiasm when you visit that seems a little too intense. The comment that's easily dismissed: "It's fine, it's just quiet here."

First: understand what you're actually dealing with

Loneliness and living alone are not the same thing. About 28% of Americans over 65 live alone — but many of them have rich social lives and feel genuinely connected. What you're watching for is the gap between the connection your parent has and the connection they want. That's what loneliness actually is.

The distinction matters because it shapes what helps. Adding more phone calls to a parent who has plenty of calls but feels unheard won't solve much. And a parent who lives alone but has a tight circle of friends and a full diary may not need intervention at all.

According to a 2024 analysis from Michigan Medicine, around one in three adults over 50 report feeling lonely — and the health consequences are serious enough that the US Surgeon General declared it a public health crisis. Chronic loneliness is linked to a 29% higher risk of heart disease, a 32% higher stroke risk, and roughly a 50% greater risk of developing dementia. This isn't a soft concern. It's worth taking seriously.

Signs your parent may be lonelier than they're letting on

Most older adults won't come right out and say they're lonely — it can feel like an admission of failure, or they don't want to worry you. Watch instead for the quieter signals:

  • →
    Calls that have nowhere to go.Long pauses, reluctance to hang up, asking the same questions twice — not from memory loss but from wanting to keep you on the line.
  • →
    Withdrawal from activities they used to enjoy.Stopped going to the book group, the church, the neighbour's for coffee. Often explained as tiredness or weather, but may run deeper.
  • →
    Sleep or appetite changes.Loneliness is closely linked with depression, which often first shows in changed sleep patterns or not bothering to eat properly when alone.
  • →
    More TV, less of everything else.Television fills silence, but it's passive. A significant increase in television hours is often a sign that the active alternatives have fallen away.
  • →
    Talking a lot about the past.Memory and reminiscence are natural — but when the past becomes the only topic and the present has nothing to report, it can signal a very small daily life.

What actually helps — and what doesn't

The honest truth about what the research shows: simply adding more contact doesn't reliably reduce loneliness. A parent who has three calls a week and still feels unseen won't feel less lonely with five calls. What matters most is the quality and meaning of the connection.

The approaches most likely to help share a few features: they're consistent (the same people, returning regularly), they involve something the older person actually enjoys (so connection is a by-product of a good activity, not the awkward stated goal), and they give your parent a sense of being genuinely interested-in, not just checked on.

  • 1
    Call more often, but keep calls shorter and more specific.A daily five-minute check-in often does more than a weekly hour-long call. And ask about something specific — what they watched, who they spoke to, what the weather was like — rather than just "how are you?" which tends to produce "fine."
  • 2
    Help them rebuild a regular social anchor.Not a calendar full of one-off events, but something that recurs: a weekly coffee with a neighbour, a standing call with a sibling, a regular class. One reliable fixture does more than a dozen events.
  • 3
    Tackle the practical barriers first.If your parent has stopped going out because they can't drive and hate asking for rides, the problem isn't loneliness — it's transport. Fix the transport and the social life may rebuild on its own. The same applies to hearing loss, which is directly linked to social withdrawal and loneliness.
  • 4
    Video calls over voice calls where possible.Seeing a face makes a significant difference — especially for older adults with hearing difficulties, who lip-read partly without realising it. Setting up a tablet propped on a stand so the camera is at eye level takes ten minutes and is worth it.
  • 5
    Consider a befriending or companion service.If your own availability is limited and your parent's social circle has thinned significantly, a consistent weekly visit or call from a companion service can provide exactly the regular, personal contact that helps most. The evidence points to one-to-one, consistent, interest-based approaches — not group activities or one-off visits.
  • 6
    Ask them what they miss — not what they need.These are often different things. "What do you need?" tends to get a dismissive answer. "What do you miss most from before?" often tells you exactly what to help rebuild.
  • 7
    Involve them in things, rather than doing things for them.Loneliness is closely tied to a loss of purpose and agency. A parent who helps plan the family holiday, teaches a grandchild something, or volunteers once a week feels like a contributor — which is different from feeling looked after.

What doesn't help as much as we think

A few common responses to a lonely parent that tend to fall short:

Suggesting they "get out more." This is advice, not help. Getting out requires knowing where to go, having transport, having the confidence, and feeling motivated — all of which are harder when you're lonely. If you want them to get out more, go with them the first few times.

One-off big events. A birthday party, a family Christmas, a single long visit can be wonderful — but they also make the return to quiet feel harder. What helps is regularity, not occasional intensity.

Pushing them to make new friends. Making friends in later life is genuinely harder — the structures that produced friends at work or school are gone. Suggesting they just meet more people is a bit like suggesting someone "just earn more money." The conditions for it need to be in place first.

One honest note

As an adult child, there is a limit to how much you can fix this — and carrying the weight of a parent's loneliness alone is a lot to hold. Using a combination of your own regular contact, local resources, and professional companion services is not giving up. It's being realistic about what one person can provide and building something more durable.
