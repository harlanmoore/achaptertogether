Meditation for sleep

Sleep problems become more common with age — and meditation is one of the better-evidenced non-drug approaches. Here's what the research shows and what to try tonight.

Poor sleep is one of the most common complaints in later life. Around 50% of older adults report some form of sleep difficulty, whether it's trouble falling asleep, frequent waking, or rising far too early. Meditation is one of the more consistently supported non-pharmaceutical approaches — and the research in older adults specifically is encouraging.

What the evidence shows

A landmark 2015 randomised trial published in JAMA Internal Medicine compared a mindfulness meditation programme against a structured sleep hygiene programme in adults with sleep disturbances. The mindfulness group showed significantly better outcomes on insomnia severity, fatigue, depression, and overall sleep quality. A 2025 scoping review in Healthcare examined studies across older adults specifically — including those with mild cognitive impairment — and found that mindfulness meditation significantly improved sleep quality and reduced insomnia severity across the board. A systematic review in Frontiers in Public Health (2023) examining mindfulness-based cognitive therapy specifically for older adults with sleep disorders confirmed meaningful improvements across multiple measures.

The evidence is not quite as strong as for Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I), which remains the gold standard first-line treatment. But meditation has a meaningful advantage: it requires no prescriptions, no specialist referral, can be done at home, costs nothing, and has wide benefits beyond sleep. It also works by a different mechanism than CBT-I, which means they can be used alongside each other.

Why sleep problems and meditation are well-matched

Most insomnia in older adults is maintained by a particular cycle: lying awake leads to frustration about lying awake, which increases arousal, which makes sleep harder, which increases frustration. The mind becomes hypervigilant about sleep — monitoring itself, calculating hours, anticipating failure.

Meditation interrupts this by training the capacity to be with present experience without reacting to it. Applied to sleep, this means lying in bed without fighting wakefulness — not pretending to be peaceful, but not escalating the resistance either. Several studies suggest it's this acceptance, rather than any direct relaxation technique, that accounts for much of the improvement.

Techniques that help with sleep specifically

Body scan before sleep

Working slowly from the feet upward, bringing gentle attention to each part of the body in turn. The combination of progressive relaxation and sustained focus tends to quieten the ruminative thinking that keeps people awake. This is one of the most studied techniques for sleep specifically.

Breath-focused relaxation

Extending the out-breath so it's longer than the in-breath (for example: in for 4 counts, out for 6 or 8). A longer exhale activates the parasympathetic nervous system — the body's rest-and-digest mode — and physiologically slows the arousal that keeps the mind alert. This can be done in bed as a wind-down practice.

The "noting" technique for middle-of-night waking

When you wake and can't return to sleep, instead of watching the clock or trying to force sleep, simply note whatever is present: "thinking," "worrying," "listening," "restless." The noting creates a gentle observer's distance from the anxious spiral, and has the added benefit of being utterly boring — which helps.

A ten-minute bedtime practice

  1. Lie on your back, arms resting beside you, legs uncrossed. Let your body settle fully into the bed.
  2. Take three slow, extended breaths: in for four counts, out for six or seven. Notice the body beginning to soften.
  3. Begin a slow body scan: bring attention to your feet, then slowly move upward — calves, knees, thighs, hips, belly, chest, shoulders, arms, hands, face. At each area, simply notice what you feel. Warmth, pressure, tingling, nothing at all. Don't try to relax; just notice.
  4. When the scan is complete, return to the breath and let it be natural. If thoughts arise, note them briefly and return.
  5. If sleep doesn't come, that's fine. Rest is also valuable. Don't monitor.

A few practical notes

A daytime meditation practice tends to improve night-time sleep even if you don't meditate specifically before bed — the general reduction in arousal and rumination carries over. If you can only do one session a day, morning or afternoon practice still benefits sleep.

The techniques above are unlikely to help if sleep problems are caused by pain, sleep apnoea, medication side effects, or significant depression — each of which warrants a conversation with your doctor. Meditation works best when the primary obstacle to sleep is mental arousal and racing thoughts, which is very common but not the only cause.
