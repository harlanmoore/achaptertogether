Meditation for grief

Meditation doesn't make grief go away — nothing does. But it can offer a steadier, kinder place from which to meet it. Here's what the evidence shows and what to try.

Grief is one of the few human experiences that resists improvement. It doesn't want to be fixed; it wants to be felt. Meditation doesn't change that — and it shouldn't promise to. What it can offer is something smaller and more honest: a slightly calmer, more grounded place from which to meet whatever is there.

What meditation can and can't do for grief

Be honest with yourself about this before you begin. Meditation is not a treatment for grief and should not be pursued with the goal of feeling less sad. Grief is a natural, necessary process. The attempt to suppress or bypass it tends to prolong it.

What meditation can do is support the secondary symptoms that often accompany grief — disrupted sleep, anxiety about the future, a restless or exhausted mind — and offer a daily period of stillness that can feel like a small refuge in a difficult season.

A 2025 systematic review in PMC (covering 13 studies, 2,097 participants) found that mindfulness-based interventions significantly reduced depression and anxiety symptoms in bereaved adults — particularly helpful given that the majority of bereaved people in the studies met criteria for probable depression at the start. Interestingly, the research also found that mindfulness was more effective at reducing secondary symptoms (depression, anxiety, stress) than at directly reducing the core grief itself, which is exactly what we'd expect: meditation eases the difficulty around grief while the grief takes its own time.

Separately, a 2025 study found that both mindfulness training and progressive muscle relaxation significantly reduced depression scores in recently widowed adults.

If grief has become overwhelming: If you're experiencing grief that feels unmanageable — persistent inability to function, thoughts of self-harm, or grief that has not shifted significantly after many months — please speak with a doctor or grief counsellor. The Institute on Aging's Friendship Line (1-800-971-0016) is also available 24 hours a day for older adults who need someone to talk to.

Practices suited to grief

Loving-kindness (Metta) meditation

This practice involves silently offering phrases of warmth and goodwill — first to yourself, then to others, including the person you are grieving. It is one of the more emotionally engaged forms of meditation, and for many bereaved people it offers a way to stay in tender connection with someone who has died, rather than simply trying to think less about them.

A simple form: sitting quietly, bring the person you're grieving to mind as vividly and warmly as you can. Then silently repeat phrases like: "May you be at peace. May you be held gently. May you be free from pain." Then turn the same phrases toward yourself: "May I find peace. May I be held gently. May I be free from pain." Do this for five to ten minutes.

Breath as anchor on hard days

Grief arrives in waves — unpredictable, sometimes overwhelming. In those moments, the breath is available as a simple, immediate anchor. Not to stop the feeling, but to stay present with it rather than being swept away. Placing a hand on the chest and taking slow, deliberate breaths while letting the feeling be there — not fighting it, not pushing it away — is a form of meditation. It says, in effect: this is happening, and I am here with it.

A short morning practice

Starting the day gently

  1. Before getting out of bed or picking up a phone, take three slow breaths and feel the weight of your body on the mattress.
  2. Bring to mind one specific thing about the person you lost that you are grateful for — a quality, a memory, a moment. Hold it gently for a minute.
  3. Set a quiet intention for the day — not a task, but a quality: "Today I'll be patient with myself." or "Today I'll notice one small beautiful thing."
  4. Take one more slow breath, and begin your day.

Grief and solitude

One of the quieter gifts of meditation during grief is that it makes solitude slightly more habitable. Many bereaved people find that the hardest time is when they are alone with a quiet house and a busy, grieving mind. A short meditation practice — even five minutes of sitting with the breath — doesn't fill the silence, but it gives it a different quality. Something is happening, something is being tended to.

This doesn't mean sitting alone with your grief is all you should do. Human contact — being heard by someone who cares — matters enormously and no meditation practice substitutes for it. The two work well together.
