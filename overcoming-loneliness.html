A practical guide

10 practical ways for seniors to overcome loneliness

Loneliness in later life is common, and it is not a personal failing. The good news is that it responds to small, steady action — and you can start with just one step.

You don't need to overhaul your life to feel more connected. A few deliberate steps, repeated over time, can quietly rebuild a sense of belonging.

The steps below are drawn from what research and real experience point to. They range from small daily habits to bigger reaches, and from in-person ideas to ones you can do from an armchair. Go at your own pace — even one of these is a good start.

1
One regular contact

Start with one regular point of contact

Don't try to fill a whole calendar at once. Pick one person and set a standing time — a Sunday phone call with a sibling, a Tuesday coffee with a neighbour. What matters most isn't how many people you see, but that something dependable recurs. A single reliable connection does more for loneliness than a dozen one-off encounters.

2
Shared enjoyment

Build connection around something you already enjoy

Connection can feel forced when it's the only goal. It comes far more naturally as a by-product of doing something you love. Join a book group, a gardening club, a choir, or a regular card game. The shared activity gives you something to talk about and a reason to keep coming back — the friendship tends to follow on its own.

3
Reach out first

Reach out first, even when it's hard

Many people quietly wait to be invited — and often, everyone is waiting at once. A short message is enough to reopen a door: "I was thinking of you. How have you been?" Reconnecting with someone you've lost touch with is usually easier than starting from scratch, and most people are genuinely glad to hear from you.

4
Weekly anchors

Put a few anchors in your week

Loneliness tends to grow in empty, unstructured time. Build small fixtures into your week that get you out of the house or onto a call — a library visit, a service at your place of worship, a class, or a walk at the same time each day. A predictable routine creates gentle, repeated chances for contact without your having to plan each one.

5
Simple technology

Make technology work for you — it's simpler than it looks

A video call can shrink the distance to a faraway grandchild or old friend. You don't need to master everything; just one tool, such as FaceTime, Zoom, or WhatsApp. Ask a family member to help you set it up once, and write the steps down on a card by your chair. Seeing a face, rather than only hearing a voice, makes a surprising difference.

6
Give something back

Give something back

Helping others is one of the most reliable ways to feel connected, because it restores a sense of purpose and puts you on equal footing — a contributor, not someone being looked after. Even small acts build bonds: reading to children, tending a community garden, or being part of a phone tree that checks in on other seniors.

7
Remove barriers

Tackle the practical barriers head-on

Sometimes the obstacle is physical rather than social. If you've been avoiding conversations because you struggle to hear, have your hearing checked — untreated hearing loss is closely linked to loneliness. If getting around is difficult, look into senior transport services, community shuttles, or volunteer ride programs. Removing one barrier can reopen many doors at once.

8
Talk to your doctor

Talk to your doctor about it

Loneliness is now recognised as a genuine health matter, and there's no shame in raising it. Your physical and mental wellbeing are closely tied to your social life, so it's a legitimate thing to bring up at an appointment. In many areas, doctors can now connect you to community groups and support through what's known as "social prescribing."

9
Befriending service

Consider a companion or befriending service

If your circle has thinned and rebuilding it feels daunting, a befriending or companion service can offer consistent, friendly contact while you find your feet. The approaches that work best share a few features: they're one-to-one, they happen regularly with the same person, and they're built around something you actually enjoy — a weekly visit, a phone call, or a shared activity to look forward to.

10
Be patient with yourself

Be patient and kind to yourself

Rebuilding connection takes time, and some attempts simply won't click — that's normal, not failure. Think of it like tending a garden: small, regular effort, and patience while things take root. Notice the small wins. One good conversation is a success worth repeating, and those repetitions are what add up.

Not sure where to begin?

Pick the one step that feels easiest — not the most important — and do it this week. Momentum matters more than perfection. If you'd like to understand why loneliness in later life is so common and what the research says, you can read our fuller guide to senior loneliness here.
